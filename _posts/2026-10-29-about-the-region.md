Residents and stakeholders on the Eastern Shore of Virginia are currently undertaking a grassroots, community-driven approach to heritage conservation and economic development in the form of a National Park Service National Heritage Area (NHA).

National Heritage Areas are designated places where historic, cultural, and national resources combine to form cohesive, nationally important landscapes. Unlike national parks, NHAs are large, lived-in landscapes. Leveraging funds and long-term support for projects, NHA partnerships foster pride of place and an enduring stewardship ethic. The benefits of NHAs include education and stewardship, an improved quality of life for citizens, and increased community engagement and pride.

Residents of the Eastern Shore of Virginia include people who have moved here and people whose families have been here since before the 1600s. The priorities in the cultural landscape and heritage for these two groups can differ. Additionally, heritage tourism is an important economic development focus of ESVA Tourism. Because of environmental threats and other economic pressures, many of the heritage sites and cultural events are in danger of disappearing. These assets need to be protected, which includes identification, public education, and a management plan. The NHA will provide federal support and technical assistance to accomplish this.

## History of the Shore

### Indigenous Roots

Eastern Shore of Virginia (ESVA) First People relations with Europeans were unique in
comparison to other tribal communities in North America. The Accomacks and
Occohannocks had “early, friendliness, loss of land, and reservations kept and then
relinquished. Only one element,” writes Helen Rountree, “so common elsewhere, was
missing on the Virginia Eastern Shore: the Accomacs and Occohannocks never went to war
with the whites. Yet they went through the same historical process—at a faster pace—that
more warlike people did.” Yes, the Accomacks here did have conflict with Europeans, but
they did not wage war.

When the first Europeans arrived on the ESVA, the First People living here were known as the Accomacks (Algonquian for “other side town”) and, further north, the Occohannocks (“other side river”).8 They lived on this narrow, seventy-mile-long peninsula situated between the Atlantic Ocean and the Chesapeake Bay. Archaeologists refer to the time period of the Accomack and the Powhatan era referenced in this Chapter as the Late Woodland period. The population of the Accomacks when Europeans arrived is unknown, but some estimated about 2,000 people. There were numerous “subtribes” in the area and the names of these changed over time. The Accomacks paid tribute to the paramount chief of the Powhatan across the Chesapeake Bay. By 1700, the Accomacks were no longer “recognizable tribal entities,” having declined due to death, assimilation, and evacuation.
