# Community Stories

As part of the feasibility study, we are working to collect local stories unique to the Eastern Shore of Virginia. Below, there are several narratives which we hope to include in the study. This is an ongoing process, and we would love your help to learn more about the economic, natural, and historic aspects of the Shore. Please fill out the form on the "Engagement" page if you have a story to share!

## The Mary N. Smith Restoration Story

The story of Mary N. Smith School is a particularly meaningful example of how local history can be preserved through community action. Constructed in 1953, the school provided secondary education for Black students during Virginia’s segregation era. Named after Mary Nottingham Smith, an influential Eastern Shore educator and advocate for Black children, the school became an important center of academic, athletic, and cultural life. Following integration in 1971, it continued operating as a middle school until closing in 2004. In 2011, former students and alumni purchased the deteriorating property from the county for $1 and began working to preserve their former school and its history. Today, the restored building serves a new generation through the Boys & Girls Club and community gatherings, demonstrating how preserving the places that shaped a community can also create opportunities for its future.

## The Move from Hog Island

For generations, the village of Broadwater on Hog Island was home to a close-knit community whose lives revolved around fishing, hunting, and the surrounding waters. As severe storms and shoreline erosion increasingly threatened the island, residents made the remarkable decision to carry their community with them. Beginning in the 1930s, families floated their homes and other buildings by barge to the mainland, relocating to several Eastern Shore communities, including Willis Wharf, Wachapreague, Oyster, and Nassawadox. Rather than leave their history behind, residents brought pieces of their island home with them, weaving Broadwater's legacy into the fabric of the peninsula. Today, these relocated buildings serve as tangible reminders of the Shore's resourcefulness and deep connection to the water. Their story is one of resilience and perseverance, demonstrating how communities adapt to a changing landscape while finding ways to preserve the places, traditions, and relationships that make them home. 

## Annual Chincoteague Seafood Festival

The Chincoteague Seafood Festival is held the first Saturday in May to promote the seafood industry on Virginia’s Eastern Shore. The festival offers fresh, local seafood and will include exciting offerings and signature dishes from local restaurants as well as old and new Eastern Shore favorites prepared by famous festival cooks, volunteers, and professionals. Shop local vendors, enjoy the live music, and eat and drink to your hearts' content. 

## The Oyster Industry

On Chincoteague, oysters were once more than a meal, but a way of life. During the 1930s, '40s, and '50s, the island was known far and wide for its oysters. Thirty shucking houses lined the community, and millions of oysters passed through their hands each season. The industry shaped the island’s livelihood and its sense of itself. But when disease devastated oyster populations in the decades that followed, a familiar part of island life began to disappear. The community found new ways to make a living from the water, turning increasingly to clam farming. Still, the memory of those oyster houses remains woven into Chincoteague’s history. In the stories of the people who worked the water and the families who gathered around its harvest, we can hear how much of the Shore’s identity is carried in the food it shares.
